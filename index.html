Part 1: Electrical inertia, the inductor

Concept and mechanical analogy

From the slides

The inductor is the passive circuit element that serves as the direct electrical analogue to mechanical mass.

To understand the inductor, one must first appreciate the physics of the magnetic field, as the behavior of the inductor is intrinsically linked to the storage of energy within this field.

Physical construction and the magnetic field

From the slides

Physically, an inductor is constructed by winding a conductive wire into a coil. This geometry is not arbitrary; it is designed to concentrate the magnetic flux produced by the current flowing through the wire.

According to Ampère's Law, any current flowing through a conductor generates a magnetic field circulating around it. By coiling the wire, these individual magnetic fields constructively interfere in the center of the coil, creating a region of high magnetic flux density.

Ampère's Law

From the slides: Ampère's Law (integral form)
\[ \oint \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 I_{enc} \]

\(\vec{l}\) is an arbitrary closed curve (usually a circle for easier analysis) around a conductor. The left-hand side measures how much of the magnetic field \(\vec{B}\) flows along (dot product) \(\vec{l}\). The equation states that its value is proportional to the current enclosed within the closed curve. The constant of proportionality is the permeability of free space \(\mu_0\).

Your handwritten notes

Decoding the symbols:

  • \(\oint\) is the "closed-loop integral" (circled in red, with a label).
  • \(\vec{l}\) = path.
  • \(d\) = "a very small something" (a tiny piece of the path).
  • \(\vec{B}\) = magnetic flux density.
  • So: \(\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{l} = Bl\).

Sketch: a circle with \(I_{enc}\) as a dot at its center; small arrows tangent to the circle show \(\vec{B}\) going around it. Red labels at the top: \(\vec{B}\) and \(d\vec{l}\) drawn along the same direction. "The circle is the path (\(l\))". Red note: "\(\vec{B}\) perfectly overlap \(\vec{l}\)" (the field is parallel to the path everywhere).

Dot-product refresher drawn on the right: two vectors \(\vec A\) and \(\vec B\) at angle \(\theta\): \(\vec A\cdot\vec B = AB\cos\theta\). When they point the same way (\(\theta = 0\)): \(\vec A\cdot\vec B = AB\).

Plain-language version, written on the page: Ampère's Law: if there is current, there is a magnetic field.

Added for study

Why \(\vec B\cdot d\vec l\) becomes just \(B\,dl\): the field is parallel to the path, so \(\cos\theta = 1\). For a circular path of radius \(r\) around a long straight wire, \(B\) has the same value everywhere on the path, so \(\oint B\,dl = B(2\pi r) = \mu_0 I\), giving \(B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\). (Your note's \(Bl\) is shorthand for this: \(l\) stands for the total path length.) \(\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{H/m}\).

V-I relationship of an inductor

Inductance

From the slides

The defining characteristic of an inductor is inductance (\(L\)), measured in Henries (H).

Inductance quantifies the device's ability to store energy in its magnetic field and is defined as the ratio of the magnetic flux linkage (\(\lambda\)) to the current (\(i\)) producing it.

\[ L = \frac{d\lambda}{di} \]

Flux linkage

From the slides

The flux linkage is the product of the magnetic flux (\(\Phi\)) and the number of turns (\(N\)) in the coil:

\[ \lambda = \Phi N \]

Added for study

The magnetic circuit is a direct analogy with a resistor circuit: mmf (\(NI\), in ampere-turns) plays the role of voltage, flux \(\Phi\) plays the role of current, and reluctance \(\mathcal{R}\) plays the role of resistance. Because flux is proportional to current, \(\lambda = N\Phi\) is proportional to \(i\), which is why the ratio \(L\) is a constant for a linear (air-core) inductor.

Faraday's Law

From the slides

The circuit behavior of the inductor is governed by Faraday's Law of Induction, which states that a time-varying magnetic flux induces an electromotive force (EMF) or voltage (\(v\)) across the coil.

\[ v = \frac{d\lambda}{dt} \]
Your handwritten notes

Beside the equation you added "or" and the alternative form:

\[ v = N\frac{d\phi}{dt} \]

Deriving \(v = L\,di/dt\)

From the slides

  1. Start from Faraday's Law and multiply and divide by \(di\) (chain rule):

    \[ v = \frac{d\lambda}{dt} = \frac{d\lambda}{di}\cdot\frac{di}{dt} \]

  2. The first factor is the definition of inductance, \(L = d\lambda/di\):

    \[ v = L\frac{di}{dt} \]
\[ v = L\frac{di}{dt} \]
Your handwritten notes

Beside the boxed result: "Small letter signifies changing with respect to time." (Lowercase \(v\) and \(i\) are time-varying quantities; uppercase such as \(I\) and \(V\) are constant or specific values.)

Added for study

Consistency check with your other form: \(\lambda = N\Phi\), so \(v = d\lambda/dt = N\,d\Phi/dt\). Both forms are the same law. The formula \(v = L\,di/dt\) uses the passive sign convention (current enters the terminal marked positive). Reading it aloud: the voltage across an inductor is proportional to how fast the current is changing, not to the current itself. Constant current means \(di/dt = 0\), so \(v = 0\).

Continuity of inductor current

Your handwritten notes

A graph of \(i\) versus \(t\): the current jumps straight up from 0 to 5 at one instant and then stays flat at 5. Red title: "physical impossibility". Beside it: "current cannot change instantaneously" (underlined). Underneath:

\[ \frac{di}{dt} = \frac{5}{0} = \infty \]

(the "= ∞" is highlighted in cyan).

From the slides

An inductor opposes changes in current just as mass opposes changes in velocity (inertia).

To change the current through an inductor instantaneously would require \(di/dt = \infty\), which implies an infinite voltage, a physical impossibility.

Therefore, current through an inductor must be a continuous function of time.

The inductor as energy storage

From the slides

Inductors are distinct from resistors in that they do not dissipate power as heat (in the ideal case); they store energy.

The instantaneous power \(p(t)\) absorbed by an inductor is the product of voltage and current.

\[ p = vi \]

Derivation of stored energy

From the slides

  1. Substitute \(v = L\,di/dt\) into \(p = vi\):

    \[ p = vi = L\frac{di}{dt}\,i = Li\frac{di}{dt} \]

  2. Energy is the integral of power over time. Use \(\tau\) as the dummy variable of integration:

    \[ W = \int_0^t p\,d\tau = \int_0^t Li\frac{di}{d\tau}\,d\tau \]

  3. The \(d\tau\) cancels (struck through on the slide), which changes the variable of integration from time to current, and the limits change from \(0\to t\) to \(0\to I\):

    \[ W = \int_0^I Li\,di = \left[\tfrac{1}{2}Li^2\right]_0^I = \tfrac{1}{2}LI^2 \]
\[ W = \frac{1}{2}LI^2 \]
Your handwritten notes (grid page)

Top left, three lines:

\[ p = Li\frac{di}{dt} \qquad\qquad w = \int p\,dt \]

The third line is hard to read; it appears to be the mechanical-work analogue \(W = \int F\,dx\).

Top middle: \(\displaystyle\int_0^t p\,d\tau = \int_0^t Li\frac{di}{d\tau}\,d\tau\) with a red arrow at \(\tau\) labeled "dummy variable for time" and the two \(d\tau\) crossed out in green.

Below it, the integration written out:

\[ w = L\int_0^t i\,di = L\int_0^I i\,di = L\left[\frac{i^2}{2}\right]_0^I = \tfrac{1}{2}LI^2 \]
Added for study

Why a dummy variable: the upper limit of the integral is already \(t\), so the variable being integrated needs a different name (\(\tau\)). Why the limits change: at \(\tau = 0\) the current is 0 and at \(\tau = t\) the current has reached \(I\), so integrating over current runs from 0 to \(I\). Note the energy depends only on the final current \(I\), not on how quickly it got there.

Part 2: Electrical elasticity, the capacitor

Concept and construction

From the slides

The capacitor is the dual of the inductor.

While the inductor opposes changes in current and stores energy in a magnetic field, the capacitor opposes changes in voltage and stores energy in an electric field.

It represents the electrical equivalent of a mechanical spring.

From the slides

A capacitor is formed by two conductive plates separated by an insulator, known as a dielectric. Ideally, no current flows through the dielectric.

Instead, when a voltage source is applied, positive charge (\(+q\)) accumulates on one plate, and an equal amount of negative charge (\(-q\)) accumulates on the opposite plate.

This separation of charge creates an electric field across the dielectric, storing potential energy.

The left plate carries \(Q+\) (plus signs) and the right plate carries \(Q-\) (minus signs), labeled "electrical charge". The circuit symbol of the capacitor is two short parallel lines with a lead out of each.

Capacitance and plate geometry

From the slides

The "capacity" of the device to store charge per unit voltage is defined as capacitance (\(C\)), measured in Farads (F).

\[ C = \frac{q}{v} \]
Your handwritten notes \[ C = \frac{q}{v} \qquad\qquad \text{Farad} = \frac{\text{Coulomb}}{\text{Volts}} \]
From the slides

The value of \(C\) is determined by the geometry of the plates and the material properties of the dielectric.

\[ C = \frac{\epsilon A}{d} \]

where \(\epsilon\) is the permittivity of the dielectric, \(A\) is the plate area, and \(d\) is the separation distance.

\[ \epsilon = \epsilon_0\,\epsilon_r \]

where \(\epsilon_0\) is the permittivity of free space (vacuum) and \(\epsilon_r\) is the relative permittivity, a value used to relate the permittivity of different materials to the permittivity of free space.

Your handwritten notes

Sketch: a circuit with a source \(I\) feeding two plates (+ + + top, − − − bottom), electric field \(\vec{E}\) arrows pointing down, and \(d\) marking the gap between the plates.

Definitions: "\(\epsilon\) = permittivity". "\(\epsilon = \epsilon_0\epsilon_r\)", with \(\epsilon_r\) labeled "relative permittivity" and \(\epsilon_0\) labeled "permittivity of free space".

Side fact on the page (the speed of light, linking the inductor and capacitor constants):

\[ c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}} = 299\,792\,458\ \text{m/s} \]
Added for study

What the geometry formula says: more plate area gives more capacitance (more room for charge); a smaller gap gives more capacitance (the plates pull charge in harder); a dielectric with a higher \(\epsilon_r\) gives more capacitance. Your note writes the speed of light with a capital "C"; it is the speed of light \(c\), not capacitance. Here \(\mu_0\) is the constant from Ampère's Law in Part 1 and \(\epsilon_0\) is the constant from this section.

Current only flows when voltage is changing

From the slides

This equation is the dual of the inductor equation.

It states that current flows into a capacitor only when the voltage across it is changing.

If the voltage is constant (\(dv/dt = 0\), i.e., DC steady state), the current is zero, and the capacitor acts as an open circuit.

Continuity of capacitor voltage

From the slides

Furthermore, just as an inductor resists instantaneous current change, a capacitor resists instantaneous voltage change. An instantaneous change in voltage would require an infinite current surge (\(i \to \infty\)).

\[ i = C\frac{dv}{dt} = C\frac{dv}{0} = \text{undefined} \]

The capacitor as energy storage

From the slides

Like the inductor, the ideal capacitor does not dissipate power; it stores it.

  1. Substitute \(i = C\,dv/dt\) into \(p = vi\):

    \[ p = vi = v\left(C\frac{dv}{dt}\right) = Cv\frac{dv}{dt} \]

  2. Integrate power over time (dummy variable \(\tau\)); the \(d\tau\) cancels, so integrate over voltage from 0 to \(V\):

    \[ W = \int_0^t p\,d\tau = \int_0^t Cv\frac{dv}{d\tau}\,d\tau = \int_0^V Cv\,dv = \left[\tfrac{1}{2}Cv^2\right]_0^V = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2 \]
\[ W = \frac{1}{2}CV^2 \]
Added for study

This is the mirror image of the inductor result \(W = \tfrac12 LI^2\): swap \(L\to C\) and \(I\to V\). Nothing in the derivation says where the energy goes when the capacitor is connected to a resistor; that is the discharge case in the RC section, where the resistor dissipates it as heat.

Part 3: Analysis of first-order circuits (RL and RC)

What makes a circuit first-order

From the slides

A "first-order" circuit contains resistance and a single equivalent energy storage element (either C or L).

The complexity of the circuit (number of resistors or sources) does not increase the order of the system; as long as all capacitors can be combined into one equivalent \(C_{eq}\) or all inductors into one \(L_{eq}\), the governing equation remains a first-order linear ODE.

The integrating factor method (general derivation)

Your handwritten notes (top of the "Series RL" grid page)

You derived the method for a general first-order linear ODE \(y' + P(x)\,y = Q(x)\) so that every circuit afterwards is just a substitution:

  1. Multiply both sides by \(e^{\int P(x)dx}\) (written in red):

    \[ e^{\int P(x)dx}\left(y' + P(x)\,y\right) = \left[Q(x)\right]e^{\int P(x)dx} \]

  2. Distribute:

    \[ y'\,e^{\int P(x)dx} + y\,e^{\int P(x)dx}\,P(x) = Q(x)\,e^{\int P(x)dx} \]

  3. Reminder in purple, the product rule: \(d(uv) = v'\,u + v\,u'\). The left side has exactly this shape.

  4. Derivative of the exponential (chain rule):

    \[ \frac{d}{dx}\left(e^{\int P(x)dx}\right) = e^{\int P(x)dx}\left(\frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\int P(x)dx\right)\right) = e^{\int P(x)}\left(P(x)\right) \]

  5. So the left side collapses into a single derivative:

    \[ \frac{d}{dx}\left(y\,e^{\int P(x)dx}\right) = Q(x)\,e^{\int P(x)dx} \]

  6. Multiply by \(dx\) and integrate both sides:

    \[ d\!\left(y\,e^{\int P(x)dx}\right) = Q(x)\,e^{\int P(x)dx}\,dx \;\Rightarrow\; \int d\!\left(y\,e^{\int P(x)dx}\right) = \int Q(x)\,e^{\int P(x)dx}\,dx \] \[ y\,e^{\int P(x)dx} = \int Q(x)\,e^{\int P(x)dx}\,dx \]

Summary (highlighted in cyan on the page):

\[ \text{integrating factor (i.f.)} = e^{\int P(x)dx} \qquad\quad y\cdot\text{i.f.} = \int Q(x)\,\text{i.f.}\,dx \]
Added for study

Recipe: (1) get the ODE into the form \(\dfrac{dy}{dt} + P\,y = Q\) with a coefficient of 1 on the derivative; (2) read off \(P\) and \(Q\); (3) compute the i.f. \(=e^{\int P\,dt}\); (4) write \(y\cdot\text{i.f.} = \int Q\cdot\text{i.f.}\,dt\); (5) integrate, add the constant \(K\), divide by the i.f.; (6) use the initial condition to find \(K\).

Transient response: natural and forced

From the slides

The response of a linear circuit to a sudden change (like a switch closing) consists of two distinct mathematical parts:

  1. Natural response
  2. Forced response

Natural response \(x_n(t)\)

This is the behavior of the circuit due solely to the internal energy stored in the capacitor or inductor. It represents the system "relaxing" to zero energy in the absence of external sources. Mathematically, this is the solution to the homogeneous differential equation.

Forced response \(x_f(t)\)

This is the behavior imposed on the circuit by the independent sources (voltage or current) for \(t > 0\). For DC sources, this is often called the steady-state response. Mathematically, this is the particular solution to the non-homogeneous differential equation.

Total response

The total response is the superposition:

\[ x_{total} = x_n + x_f \]
Added for study

Where these appear in the solutions below: the decaying exponential term, \(K e^{-t/\tau}\), is the natural response (it dies out as time passes); the constant term, \(V_S\) for the RC circuit or \(V_S/R\) for the RL circuit, is the forced (steady-state) response.

RC transient response

From the slides: the setup

Consider a series circuit consisting of a DC voltage source \(V_S\), a resistor \(R\), and a capacitor \(C\). A switch closes at \(t = 0\). The capacitor has an initial voltage \(V_0\) (which may be 0). We seek the voltage expression \(v_C\) for \(t > 0\).

Step 1: KVL gives the differential equation

From the slides

We apply Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL) to the loop for \(t > 0\).

\[ V_S - v_R - v_C = 0 \] \[ v_R + v_C = V_S \] \[ Ri + v_C = V_S \]

Substitute the capacitor current \(i = C\,dv_C/dt\):

\[ R\left(C\frac{dv_C}{dt}\right) + v_C = V_S \] \[ \frac{dv_C}{dt} + \frac{1}{RC}v_C = \frac{V_S}{RC} \]

Step 2: solve with the integrating factor

From the slides

We solve for \(v_C\) using the integrating factor method.

\[ \frac{dv_C}{dt} + \frac{1}{RC}v_C = \frac{V_S}{RC} \] \[ i.f. = e^{\int 1/RC\,dt} = e^{t/RC} \] \[ v_C\,e^{t/RC} = \int \frac{V_S}{RC}\,e^{t/RC}\,dt \]

Integrating the right side gives:

\[ v_C\,e^{t/RC} = V_S\,e^{t/RC} + K \]

Note: \(K\) is used for the arbitrary constant to avoid confusion with capacitance \(C\). Dividing by \(e^{t/RC}\):

\[ v_C = V_S + K\,e^{-t/RC} \]

Step 3: apply the initial condition

From the slides

Apply initial conditions to obtain the value for the arbitrary constant.

\[ v_C(0) = V_0 \] \[ v_C = V_S + K e^{-t/RC} \;\rightarrow\; V_0 = V_S + K e^{-0/RC} \] \[ V_0 = V_S + K \] \[ K = V_0 - V_S \]

Result

From the slides
\[ v_C = V_S + (V_0 - V_S)\,e^{-t/RC} \]

The voltage starts at \(V_0\) and exponentially decays/grows towards the final steady-state value \(V_S\). The rate of this transition is governed by \(RC\).

Linear ramps

When a current or voltage rises linearly, the derivative is constant: \(\dfrac{di}{dt} = \dfrac{\Delta i}{\Delta t}\) and \(\dfrac{dv}{dt} = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}\). (Inductor Example 1, Capacitor Exercise 1.)

Differentiating a given function

For \(i(t) = Ae^{-at}\): \(\dfrac{di}{dt} = -aA\,e^{-at}\). For \(i(t) = kt\): \(\dfrac{di}{dt} = k\). Evaluate at the requested time after differentiating. (Inductor Exercises 1 and 2.)

The three-number shortcut for any first-order circuit

The RC and RL results have the same form, so for a DC first-order circuit you only need three numbers:

\[ x(t) = x(\infty) + \big[x(0) - x(\infty)\big]e^{-t/\tau} \]
  • \(x(0)\): the initial value, from continuity (capacitor voltage and inductor current cannot jump).
  • \(x(\infty)\): the final value, from DC steady state (capacitor open, inductor short).
  • \(\tau\): \(RC\) or \(L/R\).

Match to the slides: RC has \(x = v_C\), \(x(\infty) = V_S\), \(x(0) = V_0\), \(\tau = RC\). RL has \(x = i_L\), \(x(\infty) = V_S/R\), \(x(0) = I_0\), \(\tau = L/R\).

Solving for the time to reach a target value

Isolate the exponential, then take the natural log:

\[ x = x_\infty + (x_0 - x_\infty)e^{-t/\tau} \;\Rightarrow\; e^{-t/\tau} = \frac{x - x_\infty}{x_0 - x_\infty} \;\Rightarrow\; t = \tau\ln\frac{x_0 - x_\infty}{x - x_\infty} \]

Check with RC Example 2: \(x_0 = 5\), \(x_\infty = 15\), \(x = 14\): \(t = \tau\ln\dfrac{-10}{-1} = \tau\ln 10 = 2.30\) s. ✓ (Your note gets there with \(-\ln\frac1{10}\), the same number.)

Percentage of a maximum energy

Energy goes with the square of current (or voltage), so \(\dfrac{W}{W_{max}} = \left(\dfrac{i}{I_{ss}}\right)^2\). To find when the energy reaches a fraction \(p\), first find \(i = \sqrt p\,I_{ss}\), then solve for \(t\). (RL Exercise 2.)

Unit checklist before you compute

  • Convert every prefix to base units first (mH, µH, µF, µm, ms, kΩ).
  • Use \(RC\) or \(L/R\) in seconds; an exponent must be dimensionless.
  • Keep the sign: a negative induced voltage (Inductor Exercise 1) is meaningful, not an error.
Added for study: solving any first-order problem in five steps
  1. Identify the circuit type and the storage element (\(C\) or \(L\)); find \(\tau\).
  2. Find the initial value from the circuit just before the switch moves (capacitor voltage or inductor current carries over).
  3. Find the final value with the DC steady-state rule.
  4. Write \(x(t) = x(\infty) + [x(0) - x(\infty)]e^{-t/\tau}\) (or derive it from KVL and the integrating factor, as the slides do).
  5. Substitute the requested time, or solve for the time with a natural log.
